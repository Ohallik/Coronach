# Coronach — campaign sketch

Draft 1, 2026-09-23. This is the full-game narrative proposal, not implemented campaign content. The existing slice remains the gameplay workshop. Geography, map scope and boss designs live in [WORLD_ATLAS.md](WORLD_ATLAS.md).

## The promise

**The roads between worlds are leaving. You have to teach the towns to follow.**

Cinder Halo has spent generations orbiting Vorun. Its residents think of its curved streets as permanent ground. Taren Vosk repairs the living-metal joints that hold those streets together. On a routine moon job, he and Sela discover that a supposedly abandoned station has uncoupled itself and travelled without a tug. Its inhabitants left dinner on their tables because they thought they were moving house, not evacuating.

The warp passages belong to the Choir: immense migratory organisms whose inhabited interiors have become trade routes. The Choir are approaching a seasonal departure longer than any living person's memory. The departure itself is survivable. Centuries of fixed stations, anchoring machinery and promises of permanent access have made the settlements dangerously inflexible.

The journey becomes an effort to move a chain of towns into a new, mobile neighbourhood before the routes separate. Taren and Sela can transform their own bodies into spacecraft. Their challenge is to make that freedom possible for people who cannot.

The campaign asks a personal question: **what part of a home must travel for it still to be home?** Its answer comes through people, places and difficult work rather than a final morality menu.

## Tone and dramatic engine

An energetic, strange, sometimes funny road adventure with consequential fights. The stations have breakfast queues, amateur racing leagues, terrible landlords and favourite musicians. There is enough ordinary pleasure here to make saving a neighbourhood matter. Loss has weight, but solemn speeches are rare.

Every chapter changes the physical journey: rescue a town, move it, dismantle an obstruction, earn another community's help, discover that the planned destination cannot take everyone. Friends disagree about what to leave behind. Victories open actual roads and alter familiar maps.

The Choir communicate through pressure, light and repeated movement as well as sound. Neither the player nor a character needs perfect hearing or musical pitch. Audio clues always have visual equivalents. Their intentions are inferred through observation and practical translation; they do not deliver convenient human monologues.

## People carrying the story

| Character | Wants / contradiction | Change expressed through action |
|---|---|---|
| **Taren Vosk** | Wants to keep his neighbours safe by being the person who always fixes things. He repairs systems without asking who those systems exclude. | Early, he restores a lock because it is his job. Late, he dismantles his own successful repair so someone else can leave. He learns to share responsibility rather than become everybody's indispensable rescuer. |
| **Sela Irun** | A route surveyor saving for a one-way departure. She is generous in an emergency and reluctant to promise anything beyond it. | She initially treats each town as a stop. She eventually designs a route that requires returning, and leaves a spare berth in her carefully planned life. Romance is optional subtext, not her reward or purpose. |
| **Orrin Malk** | Knows the station's people and load limits equally well. Has quietly postponed maintenance to keep rents bearable. | Must disclose what cannot safely travel. He stays alive to work through the anger; his function is not to die and motivate the heroes. |
| **Mira Tessen** | Wants to keep her shop and employees together; insists she is only protecting stock. | Her evacuation inventory gradually reveals that most of her freight allowance is other people's possessions. She still argues over money. Generosity does not erase her personality. |
| **Hal Quen** | Believes anything made well should outlive its maker. His workshop cannot be moved intact. | Builds portable benches for former apprentices, then chooses which beloved machine to disassemble himself. A quiet counterpoint to the spectacular city launch. |
| **Idra Pell** | Rauk salvage officer from Veyr's martial town. Excellent at rescue under fire; terrible at accepting help without treating it as a debt. | Joins after an operation in which the party follows her plan. Heavy brace-and-counter fighter; broad rescue-cutter flight form. Her hometown contains teachers and dissenters as well as soldiers. |
| **Oru Mere** | An Aven surveyor from the noble terraces of Merrow, publicly responsible for property records that omit whole service districts. | Gives up the hereditary right to certify boundaries, then helps others learn the work. A precise ranged duellist with tether tools, not a secretly more important prince. |
| **Meret Venn** | The public route engineer directing the Anchor Compact. Has saved thousands through competent disaster preparation. Believes a smaller guaranteed future is more honest than a larger uncertain one. | A recurring collaborator, opponent and final combatant. She chooses who bears uncertainty and will use force to enforce it. She remains accountable after defeat. |

Taren and Sela lead throughout. Idra and Oru are proposed later party members: two deployed at a time, others available at safe stops. Their recruitment is not an excuse to delay the current pair's combat quality.

## What the antagonist actually does

Meret's **Stillwater Plan** will pin one adult Choir passage in place and protect a compact corridor of settlements. She openly publishes its population capacity. It will abandon the outlying communities and destroy the passage's nursery, which is already moving through Sorrel's deep caves. Her crews begin sealing routes to make their promise of safety enforceable.

She is neither secretly possessed nor waiting to reveal that she wanted destruction. She shares accurate forecasts, lends the party equipment and rescues people the party cannot reach. Her wrongdoing is specific: destroying other people's exits to make her chosen future certain. Several Compact engineers defect; others remain because their families are inside its shelter corridor.

The party's alternative is engineered across the campaign: mobile habitats, independent power, travelling water and food systems, distributed navigation and tested emergency rendezvous. Each is demonstrated working before the finale. No relic, special bloodline or newly revealed power supplies the solution.

## Five movements

### I — A repair with nobody home (Cinder Halo, Sorrel, Gullet; roughly 4–5 hours)

**Opening scene:** Taren is repairing a market walkway during the first town's ordinary morning. A fruit seller keeps moving her stall across his safety line. Sela arrives with a fare receipt for a ship that has already left. The player learns movement and interaction while getting the bridge reopened. The station shifts a few centimetres; nobody agrees whether that is unusual.

Orrin sends the pair to Sorrel because deliveries have stopped. The outpost is occupied, not a massacre. Its workers have sealed their transmitter after a drilling machine started following signals meant for evacuation vehicles. The **Burrower** fight disables that machine-animal rig and opens the moon cave. Combat repairs a comprehensible problem.

In the **Hushwell**, regularly spaced markings first resemble a survey. At the bottom they curl around eggs. The mining operation did not uncover a weapon; it has drilled into a route nursery. Sela's charts show the same curve on the Gullet's walls. The discovery joins environmental evidence the player has already seen.

Crossing the Gullet requires freeing a navigation collar from the **Cantor**. Its weak-point fight ends with the creature opening a route rather than conveniently dying to leave a key. The existing slice's combat encounter is the starting point for this later narrative revision.

**Act turn:** at Tallow Drift, the keeper asks the party to tighten a docking line. They discover it leads to nothing. The station is moving. Its keeper is delighted; its residents have not been consulted. The first working alternative to fixed habitation is a small, messy town with a frightened population.

### II — Carrying a place (Xylos and the travelling ship-town; 6–7 hours)

Tallow's departure becomes a convoy experiment. Its hydroponic tanks are inadequate, so the party travels to jungle world **Xylos**, where the river communities grow transportable root islands. They refuse a bulk purchase that would uproot their own farms. The problem is not solved by the visitors being morally persuasive.

Instead, a storm has stranded the islands' seed carriers on the wrong bank. The player opens river routes and fights the **Floodcrown** through the exposed roots of a moving canopy. The agreement is labour and continuing access: growers travel with the convoy and retain ownership of their crop systems. A rescued seed garden becomes a revisitable space aboard the **Many Hands**, a civilian ship assembled from mismatched vessels.

During a hull breach aboard Many Hands, Meret's patrol arrives first and saves a residential section. At dinner afterward, she explains Stillwater in practical terms. Sela initially favours it: a route that stays open would let her finally leave safely. Taren's objections are still vague until he learns which moon has been designated expendable.

**Act turn:** the nursery is Sorrel. Meret does not deny it. She gives them the engineering drawings and asks for a better tested plan before her deadline. The player now knows both the danger and the opponent's proposal; the rest is not prolonged mystery-box exposition.

### III — Who gets to move? (Veyr mountains, Kiln Oath, Merrow terraces; 7–8 hours)

The convoy needs mobile engines from Veyr and navigation rights through Merrow. These chapters may be taken in either order, with dialogue acknowledging which community is already aboard.

On **Veyr**, militarised Kiln Oath has enough engines but considers passage a service obligation. Idra's rescue crews are defending stranded industrial workers while their command prepares to abandon them. The party joins a vertical extraction across avalanche galleries and a walking furnace. Idra's superior is the **Red Marshal** boss: an intimate, legible duel between larger operations. Defeating the officer stops the order, but the rescue crews decide their own next action. Idra secures engines by converting military freight allowances into civilian berths.

On **Merrow**, the noble houses own the ledgers defining which platforms count as inhabited. Oru's elegant city is literally held up by working districts absent from those records. The party enters through kitchens and lift machinery rather than winning entry to a royal ball. A public inspection becomes a running fight against the **Glass Regent**, a defensive machine following an eviction warrant. A memorable safe interlude lets the player visit an empty ceremonial theatre while a rehearsal takes place in the service lift below it.

Taren admits he installed the route lock now preventing a Veyr convoy from crossing. He knew it was exclusionary and accepted the job; this is not recovered memory. Idra does not instantly forgive him. Their next operation is spent taking apart what he built while other people direct him.

**Act turn:** Meret launches Stillwater early after a real passenger disaster. The party has gained allies but lost its expected timetable. The branching chapters converge through the consequences of that public event, not an invisible plot trigger.

### IV — The house that can leave (outer routes and the return to Cinder Halo; 5–6 hours)

At **Pale Exchange**, a water-port in a dormant comet, the party acquires independent coolant and navigational shelter. The old transfer harbour **Nacre Grave** shows why merely cutting anchors is insufficient: ships without a shared rendezvous die alone. A short optional quest recovers a living crew, not another tragic recording.

Meret offers Cinder Halo a guaranteed berth if it disconnects the outlying convoy. Orrin is tempted. The town meeting is short and grounded in known characters: Hal asks how many apprentices the berth includes; Mira demands the manifest. The player has already met the people whose entries are missing.

The party reopens the station's original separation joints. Cinder Halo was assembled from vessels; the solution has been visible in its mismatched hulls since the opening. The garden, homes and machine shops become independent craft. The old continuous promenade cannot survive separation. Moving a community does not preserve every beloved object.

**Coronach** is the local name for a leave-taking in which neighbours recite the work a place did for them before its last piece is dismantled. There is no magic in the ceremony. The game gives the player an unhurried walk through the town, with small variations earned through side stories. Then the station launches, and the score gives way to engines and familiar radio voices.

### V — A route with room to turn back (Last Mooring; 3–4 hours)

The finale alternates spacecraft and ground combat through fixed docking transitions: approach the anchor fleet, board a maintenance spine, detach a clamp, launch into the next route chamber. Rescued communities perform jobs established earlier. They are not an abstract friendship beam.

Meret pilots the **Continuance**, the engineering vessel carrying Stillwater's last anchor. The final duel begins ship-to-ship, continues across its moving exterior, and ends against her compact emergency frame inside the anchor chamber. Her attacks reuse readable machinery encountered earlier. The player defeats a formidable professional with a comprehensible objective.

Stopping her does not immediately fix the convoy. Meret provides the emergency separation sequence after defeat because withholding it would kill the same people she claimed to protect. She is taken into custody by civilian crews, not absolved by a sacrificial death.

The last playable sequence is a demanding but forgiving launch through the opening route: the player escorts the rear of the convoy, turns back for a disabled tug, and reaches the rendezvous with it. Failure restarts the local section; it does not quietly erase towns from the ending.

**Ending:** the convoy assembles into a loose new neighbourhood. There is no return to the exact old order. Taren declines the permanent emergency-director job and goes to fix a handrail. Sela flies the next survey route and actually returns. The final shot rhymes with the opening: the fruit seller has once again moved her stall across Taren's safety line, now on a street with an unfamiliar sky.

## Choices, consequences and side stories

Keep one authored resolution with tangible variations, rather than a late three-button ending. Core progress guarantees a viable convoy. Optional work changes who stays near whom, which public spaces survive, shop inventories, crew participation and the playable epilogue. Do not hide catastrophic consequences behind ordinary shopping or missed collectables.

- **Mira's second inventory:** recover three disputed consignments through different activities, then help their owners choose what goes aboard. One is a wedding table too wide for any hatch. Its owners decide to remake it together.
- **Hal's apprentices:** each repairs a different stop. Completing their stories distributes fabrication services and visibly removes Hal's monopoly on being useful.
- **The unwanted monument:** Veyr survivors disagree about carrying a memorial made from their wrecked homes. Neither position is the designated virtuous answer. The chosen arrangement changes a public square.
- **The lift choir:** Merrow's service workers want their rehearsal space back. Helping them grants a social space aboard Many Hands, not a combat buff for solving poverty.
- **Neve's return address:** deliver mundane parcels over several visits. Eventually Neve asks the party to carry her own message. The destination changes with the convoy, so finding someone is navigation as well as sentiment.
- **The last nursery survey:** optional expert flight course documents a safe seasonal route, giving the epilogue a new reachable prospect. It is not required to understand the main ending.

## Writing and staging rules

1. Every major revelation has visible evidence and changes the next action. No lore dump exists only to advertise a later mystery.
2. Each arrival starts with somebody doing something, not a ruler explaining the region. A repair, a meal, a quarrel or a rescue introduces local priorities.
3. Towns contain disagreements. Martial and noble describe institutions, not the personalities of an entire alien species.
4. Keep most compulsory conversations under ninety seconds. Optional conversation carries texture; bosses and exploration cannot depend on reading a codex.
5. Preserve moments of joy after victories. A market opening and a ridiculous local sport belong beside dangerous operations.
6. End major boss fights differently: kill a genuine predator, disable machinery, free an animal, arrest an opponent, escape a hazard. Avoid making every spectacular creature secretly misunderstood.
7. Do not promise uniqueness as an absolute. This proposal earns its identity through the moving-town premise, accountable repair work and revisitable places that physically change.

## What changes in the current slice later

Retain Cinder Halo, Sorrel, the Gullet, Tallow, Taren and Sela. Add Hushwell as the first substantial new dungeon only after locomotion, combat animation and camera clarity pass review. Rewrite the quest's missing-outpost premise as a communications shutdown, make the warp key a survey access tool, and change the Cantor victory to collar removal. These narrative changes are planned here; the existing playable quest is not silently rewritten in this pass.
